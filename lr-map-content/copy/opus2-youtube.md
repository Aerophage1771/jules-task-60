### Scene 02: The LR Question Type Map

0:44 to 1:33 (49 s) · Chapter: Why the task comes first · Frame: `scene-02-title`

**Voiceover**

Different Logical Reasoning questions ask you to do different work on an argument. If you don’t identify that work before you evaluate the choices, you can pick an answer that is true, helpful, or logically interesting and still get the question wrong.

A fact can strengthen an argument without being required by it. An assumption can be required without proving the conclusion. An answer can name a sentence’s topic without identifying its role. Those are differences in the task, and they should change how you approach the question.

So here’s the plan: the map I use for all 20 question types, then the library argument with the task changed six times, then one question to ask whenever you review a miss.

- Card 1: A fact can strengthen an argument / without / being required.
- Card 2: An assumption can be required / without / proving the conclusion.
- Card 3: An answer can name a sentence’s topic / without / identifying its role.

### Scene 03: The map: 20 types, six families

1:33 to 2:33 (60 s) · Chapter: The map: 20 types in six families · Frame: `scene-03-map`

**Voiceover**

Here’s the whole map. I organize the 20 question types into six families. The first five follow the work you do on an argument: identify its parts, describe how they work together, critique the reasoning, change its support, and then form your own conclusion from the statements you’re given.

Those five connect. Identifying the parts helps you describe the reasoning. Describing the reasoning makes its flaw easier to locate. That gap becomes the starting point for changing the support. In the fifth family your role changes: you test what the given statements support, guarantee, or rule out.

This isn’t an instruction to perform every stage on every question. Identify the requested task first. The sixth family sits apart, because those questions concern a relationship: between two speakers, between a rule and a case, or between facts that seem hard to reconcile.

**On screen**

- Headline: Six families. 20 types.
- 1 Identify the Parts: Role in Argument · Main Conclusion
- 2 Describe the Argument: Method of Reasoning · Parallel Reasoning
- 3 Critique the Argument: Flaw · Parallel Flaw
- 4 Change the Argument: Evaluate · Weaken · Strengthen · Sufficient Assumption · Necessary Assumption · Principle Strengthen
- 5 Form Your Own Argument: Fill in the Blank · Most Strongly Supported · Must Be True · Must Be False
- Divider: Separate · relationship tasks
- 6 Argument-Adjacent (gold): Agree / Disagree · Principle: Apply · Principle: Generalize · Paradox

### Scene 04: 1. Identify the Parts

2:33 to 3:30 (57 s) · Chapter: 1. Identify the Parts · Frame: `scene-04-identify`

**Voiceover**

Family one: Identify the Parts. Role in Argument and Main Conclusion both ask what a statement is doing.

Take a small argument: inspectors found cracks in the Elm Street bridge, so the bridge is unsafe, so the city should close it. “The bridge is unsafe” receives support from the cracks and then supports the closure. That makes it an intermediate conclusion. The main conclusion is the claim the author is ultimately defending: the city should close the bridge.

A sentence doesn’t become the main conclusion because it comes last or follows “therefore.” With two candidates, ask which one the author uses as a reason for the other. On Role in Argument, find the main conclusion first, then locate the quoted statement relative to it. Check both parts of the answer: the role it names, and the claim it says the statement relates to.

- Intermediate: “So the bridge is unsafe.” Gets support, gives it.
- Main conclusion (gold): “So the city should close it.” Ultimately defended.
- Test: which claim does the author use as a reason for the other?
- Watch for: A correct role paired with the wrong claim.

### Scene 05: 2. Describe the Argument

3:30 to 4:33 (63 s) · Chapter: 2. Describe the Argument · Frame: `scene-05-describe`

**Voiceover**

Family two: Describe the Argument. Once you know the parts, describe how they work together.

For Method of Reasoning, remove the topic and state the move in plain language. Without the bridge, that argument draws a judgment from an observation, then uses the judgment to support a recommendation. Then read each answer as a set of claims about the stimulus. If it says the author cites an expert and questions that expert’s motives, both things need to happen. An answer can start accurately and finish with a move the author never makes.

Parallel Reasoning asks you to find the same structure in a different argument. “Sales fell for three months, so the new menu is failing, so the restaurant should drop it” matches the bridge argument, even though it’s about food. “Traffic rose after the Elm Street bridge closed, so the closure caused it” is about the same bridge and reasons differently. Match the reasoning, not the topic.

- Parallel Reasoning, same topic, new logic (cross): “Traffic rose after the Elm Street bridge closed, so the closure caused it.”
- Watch for: A description that adds a move the author never makes.

### Scene 06: 3. Critique the Argument

4:33 to 5:32 (59 s) · Chapter: 3. Critique the Argument · Frame: `scene-06-critique`

**Voiceover**

Family three: Critique the Argument. Now ask whether the reasoning works.

For Flaw, identify the specific problem before you try to recognize it in abstract answer wording. What did the author need to establish but leave open? In the bridge argument, one gap is whether these particular cracks make the bridge unsafe. So an answer saying the argument takes for granted that the cracks make the bridge unsafe names an error it actually commits.

Then translate each answer back into the stimulus. If an answer says the argument confuses a requirement with a guarantee, point to the requirement and the claimed guarantee. Here you can’t, because the argument never states a requirement. That’s a real flaw, but not this argument’s flaw. Parallel Flaw adds a matching task: the correct answer has to fail in the corresponding way. Another bad argument isn’t necessarily a parallel bad argument.

**On screen**

- Headline: Where does the reasoning fail?

- Flaw answer A (check): “Takes for granted that the cracks make the bridge unsafe.” Found: cracks, then “unsafe.”
- Flaw answer B (cross): “Confuses a requirement with a guarantee.” Found: nothing to point to.
- Parallel Flaw: The answer must fail in the corresponding way.

**Edit and motion**

### Scene 07: 4. Change the Argument

5:32 to 6:24 (52 s) · Chapter: 4. Change the Argument · Frame: `scene-07-change`

**Voiceover**

Family four: Change the Argument. Six types live here: Evaluate, Weaken, Strengthen, Sufficient Assumption, Necessary Assumption, and Principle Strengthen. They often begin from the same gap and ask you to do different things with it.

Stay with the bridge. Asking how deep the cracks go would help you evaluate the argument. A finding that the cracks are only surface wear would weaken it. A finding that they run through the bridge’s main supports would strengthen it. Same gap, three different jobs.

That shared starting point doesn’t make the tasks interchangeable. Helpful, required, and sufficient are different standards, and the bridge argument is too loose to show exactly where they separate. So once the map is done, I’ll take one argument with numbers in it through all six tasks.

### Scene 08: 5. Form Your Own Argument

6:24 to 7:35 (71 s) · Chapter: 5. Form Your Own Argument · Frame: `scene-08-form`

**Voiceover**

Family five: Form Your Own Argument. Here the task concerns what the statements support, complete, or rule out.

Take three statements. Every bridge on Route 9 was inspected this year. Most bridges on Route 9 are over 50 years old. The Pine Street bridge is on Route 9. Now four claims about the Pine Street bridge. “It was inspected this year” is guaranteed. “It is probably over 50 years old” is supported, but not guaranteed. “It was not inspected this year” is contradicted. And “it passed its inspection” is merely possible: the statements never say.

Must Be True asks for a guarantee, so try to make the answer false while the stimulus stays true. Most Strongly Supported asks for the claim with the best support, and you should be able to point to it. Must Be False asks for a claim that can’t coexist with the facts. Something the stimulus never discusses isn’t automatically false. It may simply be unknown. Fill in the Blank needs one more check first: what job is the missing statement doing?

**On screen**

- Eyebrow: Family 5 of 6 · Form Your Own Argument (tracker: 5 current)
- Headline: What do the facts establish?
- The statements: Every bridge on Route 9 was inspected this year. Most bridges on Route 9 are over 50 years old. The Pine Street bridge is on Route 9.
- Fill in the Blank: First name the job the missing statement does.
- Guaranteed · Must Be True: “It was inspected this year.”
- Supported · Most Strongly Supported: “It is probably over 50 years old.”
- Contradicted · Must Be False: “It was not inspected this year.”
- Possible · not false, just unknown (gold): “It passed its inspection.”

**Edit and motion**

### Scene 09: 6. Argument-Adjacent

7:35 to 8:34 (59 s) · Chapter: 6. Argument-Adjacent · Frame: `scene-09-adjacent`

**Voiceover**

Family six: Argument-Adjacent. These four types involve relationships that need their own checks.

For Agree or Disagree, establish each speaker’s position on the exact statement in the answer. Silence isn’t disagreement. If one speaker’s view is unknown, you don’t yet have the required relationship. For Principle: Apply, break the rule into its conditions and its result, then check the case against each condition. If a rule says a bridge with cracked main supports must close, a bridge with cracks somewhere else doesn’t trigger it. Meeting most of a rule’s requirements isn’t meeting all of them.

Principle: Generalize reverses the direction. Start with the case and find the broader rule it illustrates. A principle about the same subject can still misdescribe the case. For Paradox, state what’s surprising: we’d expect this, but instead that happened. A correct explanation lets both facts remain true and makes their coexistence less surprising.

**On screen**

- Eyebrow: Family 6 of 6 · Argument-Adjacent (tracker: 6 current)
- Headline: Check both sides.
- Agree / Disagree: Speaker A and Speaker B (two-way arrow). Pin down both views. Silence isn’t disagreement.
- Principle: Apply: Rule, then case (arrow). Check the case against every condition.
- Principle: Generalize: Case, then rule (arrow). Same subject isn’t enough.
- Paradox: Fact A plus fact B. Explain both. Deny neither.

**Edit and motion**

### Scene 11: Name the gap

9:08 to 9:55 (47 s) · Frame: `scene-11-gap`

**Voiceover**

Before you read any question stem, find what the premises leave open. The saving is fixed at $4,000. The only cost that can move is maintenance. Call the added annual maintenance cost M. The net change in annual costs is M minus $4,000.

Put that on a line. If M is below $4,000, total costs fall and the conclusion holds. At exactly $4,000, costs break even, and breaking even isn’t a reduction. Above $4,000, costs rise. So the conclusion holds exactly when M is below $4,000. All six tasks start from that gap, and each one asks you to do something different with it.

- Gold card, conclusion holds when: M is below $4,000.
- Number line, $0 to $6,000: green zone “Costs fall”, red zone “No reduction”, marker “Break even at $4,000”.

### Scene 12: Task 1: Evaluate

Proposed answer: how much will Model L increase the library’s annual maintenance costs? Try contrasting answers. If it’s $1,000, the saving exceeds the added cost, and total costs fall. If it’s $5,000, the added cost exceeds the saving, and total costs rise. Opposite answers lead to opposite assessments. That’s what makes the question useful.

### Scene 15: Task 4: Necessary Assumption

11:51 to 12:46 (55 s) · Chapter: Task 4: Necessary Assumption · Frame: `scene-15-necessary-assumption`

**Voiceover**

Task four: Necessary Assumption. The answer has to be something the argument requires. Test it by negating it precisely.

Proposed answer: Model L will add less than $4,000 a year in maintenance costs. The negation is $4,000 or more. At exactly $4,000, costs break even. Above it, they rise. Either way there’s no reduction, so the argument needs this answer. Here the negation defeats the conclusion outright. You don’t usually have to go that far: it’s enough that the reasoning loses support it requires.

Compare a stronger claim: maintenance won’t rise at all. The argument doesn’t need that, because a $3,000 increase would still leave a saving. In this numerical example, the under-$4,000 threshold is sufficient as well as necessary. Necessity and sufficiency are properties that can overlap.

**On screen**

- Eyebrow: Task 4 of 6 · Necessary Assumption (tracker: 4 current)
- Headline: The argument must require it.
- Stem: “Which one of the following is an assumption required by the argument?”
- Proposed answer: “Model L will add less than $4,000 a year in maintenance costs.”
- Negate it: $4,000 or more: no reduction. Verdict (check): Required.
- Number line: green range “Required: under $4,000”, red range “Negation: $4,000 or more”.

**Edit and motion**

- Band alone first (1.5-second hold), then the answer card.

Task five: Sufficient Assumption. Add the answer to the premises, and the conclusion has to follow with no gap left.

This is the answer from the opening. It’s sufficient, but it isn’t necessary: a $3,000 increase would also leave a saving. A sufficient condition can go further than the argument needs.

Here’s how to use the map on your next question. Identify the type and state the task in ordinary language. Then choose the tool. Sometimes that’s a full prephrase.
