## Caption

Before you decide whether an LSAT answer is good, decide what it needs to do.

An answer can be true, helpful, or logically interesting and still miss the question. A statement that strengthens an argument might not be necessary to it. A necessary assumption might leave the conclusion unproven.

The image holds one argument fixed so you can see the difference. Replacing a library’s lights with Model L fixtures will cut its annual electricity bill by exactly $4,000, and maintenance is the only other cost that may change. The author concludes that total annual operating costs will fall.

Strengthen needs a fact that improves the support. If three comparable libraries with the same fixtures saw maintenance rise less than $1,000, that supports a low estimate here. It doesn’t guarantee one.

Necessary Assumption needs something the argument requires. Added maintenance has to cost less than $4,000 a year. Negate that, and $4,000 or more would mean no reduction.

Sufficient Assumption needs a statement that makes the conclusion follow. A $1,000 cap on added maintenance guarantees at least $3,000 in savings. The argument doesn’t need a cap that tight, though: a $3,000 increase would still allow savings.

Helpful, required, and sufficient are different standards. So are possible, supported, guaranteed, and contradicted. The LR Question Type Map organizes all 20 types into six families, with a method and a wrong-answer check for each. Use it to identify the task before choosing, then return to it during review to name the standard your answer missed. The full map and the free guide are linked in the first comment.

Try one in the comments. Suppose Model L adds no maintenance cost at all. Is that required for the conclusion, sufficient for it, or both? Tell me which test you used to decide.

## Reply with the answer (post after a few people have answered)

Sufficient, not required. Add it to the premises first: the bill falls by $4,000 and nothing else rises, so total costs fall. Then negate it: Model L adds some maintenance cost. If that cost is $3,000, total costs still fall by $1,000, so the argument never needed the stronger claim.

One more check: “Added maintenance will cost less than $4,000 a year” is the required answer in the image, and in this example it is also sufficient. A statement can meet more than one standard. The question stem tells you which one you need.

## What changed from the source copy, and why

- The caption opens with the source’s first line, which already works, then adds the demonstration the source copy lacked: one argument, three answers, three standards. The voice’s teaching pattern asks for a rule and a worked example, not just the distinction.
- The links moved to the first comment so the caption can end on a specific discussion prompt (Facebook’s register), and the source’s duplicate links (body and first comment) became one set.
- The prompt has a definite answer (sufficient, not required), so the reply can teach the negation test and the point the source makes about overlap: a statement can meet more than one standard, and the stem decides which one you need.
- The first comment says the library argument is an original teaching example, not an LSAT question.
- The images avoid the ≠ and arrow characters, which the brand fonts do not include, and every box is enclosed on all sides (the original map used top stripes).
