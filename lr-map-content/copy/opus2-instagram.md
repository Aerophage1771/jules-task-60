## Caption

```text
A statement can strengthen an argument without being necessary to it. It can be necessary without being enough to prove the conclusion.

If those standards blur together, the same answer can seem right for three different questions. Slide 1 shows one: a $1,000 cap on added maintenance meets the standard for Strengthen and for Sufficient Assumption, and fails it for Necessary Assumption.

I organized the 20 Logical Reasoning question types into six families. The first five follow the work you do on an argument: identify its parts, describe it, critique it, change it, and draw a conclusion yourself. The sixth covers two speakers, rules and cases, and apparent conflicts.

Slides 7 to 11 hold one library’s cost argument fixed and change the task six times. The Weaken examples gave you several ways to attack reasoning. This shows where that skill fits and what changes when the task changes.

For your next missed question, state the task in one sentence. Then explain which part of that task your selected answer failed.

Save the map for review. The full article and free guide are linked in the first comment.
```

**Slide 03**

Family 1, Identify the Parts: Role in Argument and Main Conclusion. Headline: Ask which claim the author uses as a reason for the other. Example stimulus with each part highlighted. “The clinic should hire a second nurse.” is the main conclusion. “Patients now wait more than an hour,” is the premise. “so the current staff cannot meet demand.” is the intermediate conclusion. Three boxes show the support running from Premise, long waits, to Intermediate, staff can’t meet demand, to Main, hire a second nurse. Text: The shortage is a reason to hire, not the reverse. So the first sentence is the main conclusion, even though “so” comes later. Watch for: An intermediate conclusion presented as the main one. On Role in Argument, check both parts of an answer: the role it names and the claim it says the statement supports. Slide 3 of 14.

**Slide 04**

Family 2, Describe the Argument: Method of Reasoning and Parallel Reasoning. Headline: Remove the topic. Describe the move. Two example arguments side by side. A museum: Every museum in the district closes on Mondays. The Hale is one of them. So the Hale closes on Mondays. A forest: Every trail in Pine Forest closes at dusk. Ridge Trail is one of them. So Ridge Trail closes at dusk. A dark box, Same move, different topics: A rule about a whole group, applied to one member of it. Method of Reasoning: state that move in plain language. Parallel Reasoning: find it in a different argument. If several choices fit, compare the conditions, the direction of support, and the force of the conclusion. Watch for: Matching topics or familiar words. Two arguments about doctors can reason differently. Slide 4 of 14.

**Slide 05**

Family 3, Critique the Argument: Flaw and Parallel Flaw. Headline: Name the gap before you read the flaw answers. Example stimulus: A deck must have a permit to pass inspection. Ana’s deck has a permit. So Ana’s deck will pass. The gap: A permit is required to pass. Nothing says it is enough. A dark box, The answer says: “The argument treats a requirement as a guarantee.” An arrow points to a box headed Point to both. Requirement: “must have a permit to pass.” Guarantee: “So Ana’s deck will pass.” Watch for: A real flaw that this argument does not commit. In Parallel Flaw, the answer must fail in the corresponding way. Another bad argument is not necessarily a parallel one. Slide 5 of 14.

**Slide 06**

Family 4, Change the Argument. Headline: Helpful, required, and sufficient are different standards. Text: These six types often begin from the same gap. They ask you to do different things with it. A table with the columns Question type and The answer must. Evaluate: help you judge the reasoning. Weaken: reduce the support for the conclusion. Strengthen: improve the support for the conclusion. Sufficient Assumption: make the conclusion follow. Necessary Assumption: be something the argument requires. Principle Strengthen: be a general rule that helps justify the conclusion. Watch for: Using “this helps” as the standard for every question in the family. Next: one argument, six tasks. Slide 6 of 14.

**Slide 07**

Family 4 worked example. Headline: One argument, six tasks. First, find what it leaves open. The argument, invented for teaching. Premise: Replacing a library’s conventional lights with Model L fixtures will cut its annual electricity bill by exactly $4,000. Premise: All other annual operating costs stay the same, except that Model L may add maintenance costs. Conclusion: So the switch will reduce the library’s total annual operating costs. The gap, how much will maintenance rise: a bar of added maintenance from $0 to $6,000, green and labeled Costs fall below $4,000, red and labeled Costs rise above it, with $4,000 marked as breaking even. Net annual change equals added maintenance minus $4,000. The conclusion holds only if added maintenance stays under $4,000. Each task on the next three slides works on that gap. Slide 7 of 14.

**Slide 08**

Family 4 worked example. Headline: Evaluate asks a question. Weaken adds a fact. The maintenance bar again, with two markers: $1,000 in the green zone, labeled holds, and $5,000 in the red zone, labeled fails. Task 1 of 6, Evaluate: “How much will Model L add to annual maintenance?” If $1,000, the conclusion holds. If $5,000, it fails. Contrasting answers change the assessment, so the question is useful. Task 2 of 6, Weaken: “Model L will add $5,000 a year in maintenance.” That more than cancels the $4,000 saving: total costs rise by $1,000. This weakener happens to disprove the conclusion. Weakening does not always require disproof. Slide 8 of 14.

**Slide 09**

Family 4 worked example. Headline: Strengthen adds support. A principle must fit the case. Task 3 of 6, Strengthen: “Three libraries with comparable operating hours and the same fixtures had maintenance increases under $1,000.” That supports a low estimate here. It does not guarantee one, because this library’s conditions could differ. Task 4 of 6, Principle Strengthen: “Libraries that replace conventional lights with Model L add no more than $1,000 in annual maintenance.” Three boxes in a chain. The facts: this library is making that switch. The rule applies: added cost is $1,000 at most. Conclusion: costs fall by $3,000 or more. A rule about a different fixture model would not apply merely because its result looks useful. Slide 9 of 14.

**Slide 10**

Family 4 worked example. Headline: Negate it to test necessary. Add it to test sufficient. A chart of added annual maintenance from $0 to $6,000. A long blue bar from $0 to $4,000 is labeled Required: under $4,000. A short gold bar from $0 to $1,000 is labeled Enough: no more than $1,000. A dashed line at $3,000 falls inside the blue bar and outside the gold one. Task 5 of 6, Necessary Assumption: “Model L will add less than $4,000 a year in maintenance.” Negate it: $4,000 or more. Then total costs do not fall, so the argument requires it. Task 6 of 6, Sufficient Assumption: “Model L will add no more than $1,000 a year in maintenance.” Add it: at least $3,000 saved, so the conclusion follows. It is not necessary: a $3,000 increase, the dashed line, still saves $1,000. Slide 10 of 14.

**Slide 11**

Family 4 worked example. Headline: The stem decides which standard counts. A table checks three statements against Strengthen, Sufficient and Necessary. “Model L will add less than $4,000 a year”: yes, yes, yes. Highlighted as the answer on slide 1, “Model L will add no more than $1,000 a year”: yes, yes, no. “Three comparable libraries had increases under $1,000”: yes, no, no. Text: A statement may meet more than one standard. The first one here is both necessary and sufficient. The $1,000 cap guarantees savings, but savings do not require that tight a cap. The rule: Before you compare choices, state which property the stem asks for. Increased support is enough unless the question asks you to make the conclusion follow. Slide 11 of 14.

**Slide 12**

Family 5, Form Your Own Argument: Fill in the Blank, Most Strongly Supported, Must Be True, Must Be False. Headline: Supported is not guaranteed. Unknown is not false. Stimulus: Most members of the chess club are seniors. Jordan is a member of the chess club. Four claims, each with its relationship to the facts. Possible, the facts leave it open: “Jordan joined the club this year.” Never discussed, so it is unknown. Supported, Most Strongly Supported: “Jordan is a senior.” Likely, since most are. Not guaranteed. Guaranteed, Must Be True: “At least one member is a senior.” Try to make it false. You can’t. Contradicted, Must Be False: “No club member is a senior.” It cannot coexist with the facts. Fill in the Blank: Name the blank’s job first. A conclusion needs support from what came before; a missing premise must do the work the argument requires. Slide 12 of 14.

**Slide 13**

Family 6, Argument-Adjacent: Agree / Disagree, Principle: Apply, Principle: Generalize, Paradox. Headline: Check both sides of the relationship. Four cards. Agree / Disagree, shown as Speaker A and Speaker B: Pin down each speaker’s view on the exact statement. Trap: Treating one speaker’s silence as disagreement. Principle: Apply, shown as rule to case: Check the case against each of the rule’s conditions. Trap: A case that meets only some of the conditions. Principle: Generalize, shown as case to rule: Start with the case. Find the broader rule it illustrates. Trap: A same-subject rule that misstates the case. Paradox, shown as fact plus fact: Name the expectation and the surprising result. Trap: Denying a fact instead of explaining both. Slide 13 of 14.
