# YouTube storyboard: The LR Question Type Map (Opus track)

Runtime: 14:32 across 22 scenes. Frames are 1920×1080 and live in `src/opus-youtube.html` (built to `dist/opus-youtube.html`); each frame shows the final build state of its scene. Arc: preview (scenes 1 to 3), teach the six families (4 to 10), demonstrate on one library argument (11 to 18), recap and next step (19 to 22).

## Production notes

- Each frame shows the final build state of its scene. Rebuild the reveals in the edit in the order the notes give, using crop masks on the exported PNG, or rebuild the elements as layers.
- Timings assume a voiceover pace of about 156 words a minute plus a 1.5 second visual beat per scene. If the recorded read runs long or short, move the chapter times in the description to match the final cut before you publish.
- Cut style: straight cuts inside a chapter; a 12-frame push left at each chapter change (into scenes 4, 11 and 19).
- Scenes 12 to 18 share one locked layout: the header band, the two cards and the number line stay in identical positions, so the line reads as one object across all six tasks.
- If you use a music bed, drop it out for scenes 12 to 18 so the dollar figures are easy to follow.
- Upload captions made from this script rather than relying on automatic captions, which may mishear “Model L”, the dollar figures and the question-type names.
- Keep key content out of the bottom 100 px of each frame, where YouTube shows the player controls. Only the logo sits there.

### Scene 05: 1. Identify the Parts

2:27 to 3:16 (49 s) · Chapter: The map · Frame: `scene-05-identify`

**Voiceover**

Family one: Identify the Parts. Role in Argument and Main Conclusion both ask for a statement’s job.

On Main Conclusion, you need the claim the author is ultimately defending. A sentence doesn’t become the main conclusion because it comes last or follows “therefore.” An intermediate conclusion receives support and then helps support another claim. So take two candidate conclusions and ask which one the author uses as a reason for the other.

On Role in Argument, find the main conclusion first, then locate the quoted statement relative to it. Check both parts of the answer: the role it names, and the claim it says the statement relates to. You’re identifying a job, not choosing the statement that sounds most important or true.

- Flow: Premise (supports a claim) → Intermediate conclusion (receives support, then gives it) → Main conclusion (what the author ultimately defends)
- Test: which claim does the author use as a reason for the other?
- Watch for: A correct role paired with the wrong claim.

### Scene 06: 2. Describe the Argument

3:16 to 4:00 (44 s) · Chapter: The map · Frame: `scene-06-describe`

**Voiceover**

Family two: Describe the Argument. Once you know the parts, describe how they work together.

For Method of Reasoning, remove the topic and state the move in plain language. The author might eliminate alternatives, compare two cases, or apply a rule. Then read each answer as a set of claims about the stimulus. An answer can start accurately and finish with a move the author never makes.

Parallel Reasoning asks you to find the same structure in a different argument. Match the reasoning, not the topic. Two arguments about doctors can reason differently, and an argument about a museum can use the same reasoning as one about a forest.

### Scene 07: 3. Critique the Argument

4:00 to 4:44 (44 s) · Chapter: The map · Frame: `scene-07-critique`

**Voiceover**

Family three: Critique the Argument. Now you ask whether the reasoning works.

For Flaw, identify the specific problem before you try to recognize it in abstract answer wording. What did the author need to establish but leave open? Then translate each answer back into the stimulus. If an answer says the argument confuses a requirement with a guarantee, point to the requirement and to the claimed guarantee. If you can’t locate that error in the reasoning, knowing the name of the fallacy isn’t enough.

Parallel Flaw adds a matching task. The correct answer has to fail in the corresponding way. Another bad argument isn’t necessarily a parallel bad argument.

### Scene 08: 4. Change the Argument

4:44 to 5:36 (52 s) · Chapter: The map · Frame: `scene-08-change`

**Voiceover**

Family four: Change the Argument. Six types live here: Evaluate, Weaken, Strengthen, Sufficient Assumption, Necessary Assumption, and Principle Strengthen. They often begin from the same gap, and they ask you to do different things with it.

Evaluate asks for information that would help you judge the reasoning. Weaken and Strengthen ask you to reduce or improve the support. Sufficient Assumption asks for a statement that makes the conclusion follow. Necessary Assumption asks for something the argument requires, and you test it by negating the candidate. Principle Strengthen asks for a general rule that helps justify the conclusion.

So “this helps” can’t be your standard for every question in this family. Helpful, required, and sufficient are different, and the worked example later in this video puts all six on one argument.

**On screen**

- Eyebrow: Family 4 of 6 · Change the Argument (tracker: 4 current)
- Headline: Helpful ≠ required ≠ sufficient.
- Evaluate: Find what helps you judge it.
- Weaken: Reduce the support.
- Strengthen: Improve the support.
- Sufficient Assumption: Make the conclusion follow.
- Necessary Assumption: Find what the argument requires.
- Principle Strengthen: Supply a rule that helps justify it.
- Watch for: “This helps” as the standard for every question.

**Edit and motion**

- The headline’s two ≠ signs draw on with “Helpful, required, and sufficient are different.”

### Scene 09: 5. Form Your Own Argument

5:36 to 6:32 (56 s) · Chapter: The map · Frame: `scene-09-form`

**Voiceover**

Family five: Form Your Own Argument. Here your role changes. You test what the given statements support, complete, or rule out.

On Most Strongly Supported, look for the claim with the best support in the information provided, and point to that support. A claim can be plausible in the real world without being supported by this stimulus. Must Be True asks for a guarantee: try to make the answer false while leaving the stimulus true. If you can, it isn’t required by the facts. Must Be False asks for an answer that can’t coexist with the given information. Something the stimulus never discusses isn’t automatically false. It may simply be unknown.

On Fill in the Blank, identify what the missing statement is doing before you choose a completion. Possible, supported, guaranteed, and contradicted are four different relationships to the evidence.

**On screen**

- Eyebrow: Family 5 of 6 · Form Your Own Argument (tracker: 5 current)
- Headline: What do the facts establish?
- Most Strongly Supported: Supported: you can point to its support.
- Must Be True: Guaranteed: holds whenever the facts hold.
- Must Be False: Contradicted: can’t coexist with the facts.
- Fill in the Blank: Completes: performs the blank’s job.
- Watch for: Not discussed isn’t false. It may simply be unknown.

**Edit and motion**

- Table rows reveal top to bottom as each type is read. The bold relationship word in each row lands a beat before its test.

### Scene 10: 6. Argument-Adjacent

6:32 to 7:21 (49 s) · Chapter: The map · Frame: `scene-10-adjacent`

**Voiceover**

Family six: Argument-Adjacent. These four types involve relationships that need their own checks.

For Agree or Disagree, establish each speaker’s position on the exact statement in the answer. Silence isn’t disagreement. For Principle: Apply, break the rule into its conditions and result, then check the case against each condition. Meeting most of a rule’s requirements isn’t meeting all of them. Principle: Generalize reverses the direction. Start with the case and identify the broader rule it illustrates. A principle about the same subject can still misdescribe the case.

For Paradox, state what’s surprising: we’d expect this, but instead that happened. A correct explanation lets both facts remain true and makes their coexistence less surprising. In all four, check both sides of the relationship.

**On screen**

- Eyebrow: Family 6 of 6 · Argument-Adjacent (tracker: 6 current)
- Headline: Check both sides.
- Agree / Disagree: Speaker A ↔ Speaker B. Silence isn’t disagreement.
- Principle: Apply: Rule → case. Check every condition.
- Principle: Generalize: Case → rule. Same subject isn’t enough.
- Paradox: Fact A ↔ fact B. Explain both. Deny neither.
- Watch for: Ignoring one side of the relationship.

**Edit and motion**

- Cards build in reading order. In each, the relationship line draws its arrow first, then the check line fades in.

### Scene 12: Name the gap

7:54 to 8:31 (37 s) · Chapter: One argument, six tasks · Frame: `scene-12-gap`

The premises leave one number open: how much Model L adds to annual maintenance. Call it M. The net change in annual costs is M minus $4,000.

Put that on a line. Below $4,000, total costs fall and the conclusion holds. At exactly $4,000, costs break even, and breaking even isn’t a reduction. Above $4,000, costs rise. So the conclusion holds only when the added maintenance cost is less than $4,000.

That threshold is the gap. All six tasks work on it, and each one does something different with it.

### Scene 16: Task 4: Necessary Assumption

10:15 to 10:54 (39 s) · Chapter: One argument, six tasks · Frame: `scene-16-necessary`

Task four: Necessary Assumption. Proposed answer: Model L will add less than $4,000 to the library’s annual maintenance costs.

### Scene 17: Task 5: Sufficient Assumption

10:54 to 11:35 (41 s) · Chapter: One argument, six tasks · Frame: `scene-17-sufficient`

**Voiceover**

Task five: Sufficient Assumption. Proposed answer: Model L will add no more than $1,000 to the library’s annual maintenance costs.

Add it to the premises. The library saves $4,000 on electricity and adds at most $1,000 in maintenance, so it saves at least $3,000 a year. With all other costs fixed, total costs must fall. The conclusion follows.

But the cap isn’t necessary. If maintenance rose $3,000, the conclusion would still hold. The $1,000 cap is one sufficient condition, not a condition the argument must have. The question asks whether the answer is enough, not whether it’s the weakest possible guarantee.

**Voiceover**

Here’s how to use the map in practice. When you begin a question, identify its type and state the task in ordinary language. Then choose the tool: sometimes a full prephrase, sometimes an abstract structure, sometimes a gap, a rule, or a contradiction to test against the choices.
