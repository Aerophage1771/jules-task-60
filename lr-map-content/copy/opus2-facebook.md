## Caption (post with image 1)

Before you decide whether an LSAT answer is good, decide what it needs to do.

Helpful, necessary, and sufficient are different standards. So are possible, supported, guaranteed, and contradicted. If those standards blur together, the same answer can seem right for three different questions.

The image holds one argument fixed: Model L lights will cut a library’s annual electricity bill by exactly $4,000, and nothing else changes except possible added maintenance. The conclusion, that total annual operating costs will fall, holds only if that added maintenance stays under $4,000.

Sufficient Assumption needs a statement that makes the conclusion follow, so add the answer to the premises and check. A $1,000 cap on added maintenance passes: it guarantees at least $3,000 in savings. The argument doesn’t need a cap that tight, though. A $3,000 increase would still save money.

Necessary Assumption needs something the argument requires, so negate the answer and check. “Less than $4,000” passes: $4,000 or more means no reduction. In this example it is sufficient too. A statement can meet more than one standard, and the question stem tells you which one you need. On real questions, the negation doesn’t have to make the conclusion false. Ask whether the reasoning still has the support it requires.

Helpful is a third standard. If three comparable libraries with the same fixtures saw maintenance rise less than $1,000, that strengthens the argument without guaranteeing this library’s cost, and the argument doesn’t need it.

I organized all 20 LR question types into six families, with a method and a wrong-answer check for each. Use the map to identify the task before choosing, then return to it during review to name the standard your answer missed. The map and the free guide are linked in the first comment.

Your turn, from the last row of the image: “Added maintenance will be less than $5,000.” Required, sufficient, both, or neither? Tell me which test you used.

## Reply with the answer (attach image 3, after a few people have answered)

Required, not sufficient.

Negate it first: added maintenance is $5,000 or more. Total costs then rise by at least $1,000, so the argument can’t work without the statement. Now add it to the premises instead. An increase of $4,500 is still less than $5,000, and it raises total costs by $500, so the statement doesn’t make the conclusion follow. On the chart, its bar covers the whole zone where costs fall, then runs past it.

That completes the set. The $1,000 cap is sufficient but not required. “Less than $4,000” is both. “Less than $5,000” is required but not sufficient. Same argument, three statements, two tests: the question stem tells you which test the right answer has to pass.

## Alt text: image 1 (fb-01-sufficient-vs-required)

Chart titled “Sufficient and required are different standards,” an original teaching example. The argument: switching a library to Model L lights will cut its annual electricity bill by exactly $4,000, and all other annual operating costs stay the same, except that Model L may add maintenance costs. Conclusion: total annual operating costs will fall. A scale of added annual maintenance runs from $0 to $6,000. Below $4,000, total costs fall; at $4,000 or more, there is no reduction. Each answer is drawn as a bar showing the amounts it allows. “No more than $1,000” stops well inside the zone where costs fall, marked sufficient, not required. “Less than $4,000” fills that zone exactly, marked sufficient and required. “Less than $5,000” covers the zone and runs past $4,000, marked with question marks for the reader to decide. The key: sufficient means every amount the answer allows cuts costs, so adding it makes the conclusion follow; required means the answer allows every amount that cuts costs, so negating it leaves no reduction possible. ## Alt text: image 2 (fb-02-six-families)

Chart titled “20 types. Six families.” from the LR Question Type Map. 01 Identify the Parts asks what each statement is doing: Role in Argument, Main Conclusion. 02 Describe the Argument asks how the parts work together: Method of Reasoning, Parallel Reasoning. 03 Critique the Argument asks where the reasoning fails: Flaw, Parallel Flaw. 04 Change the Argument is highlighted as the family in this post, with the note that helpful, required, and sufficient are different standards: Evaluate, Weaken, Strengthen, Sufficient Assumption, Necessary Assumption, Principle Strengthen, with Strengthen and the two assumption types marked. 05 Form Your Own Argument asks what the facts support or rule out: Fill in the Blank, Most Strongly Supported, Must Be True, Must Be False. 06 Argument-Adjacent, labeled relationship tasks, says to check both sides of the relationship: Agree / Disagree, Principle: Apply, Principle: Generalize, Paradox.

Answer chart titled “‘Less than $5,000’ is required, not sufficient.” It repeats the library argument and the scale of added annual maintenance from the post image, where total costs fall only below $4,000.
