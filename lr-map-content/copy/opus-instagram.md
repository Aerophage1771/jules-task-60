## Caption

```text
A statement can strengthen an argument without being necessary to it. It can be necessary without being enough to prove the conclusion.

If those standards blur together, the same answer can seem right for three different questions.

I organized the 20 Logical Reasoning question types into six families. The first five follow the work you do on an argument: identify its parts, describe it, critique it, change it, and draw a conclusion yourself. The sixth covers two speakers, rules and cases, and apparent conflicts.

Slides 7 to 10 hold one library’s cost argument fixed and change the task. The most useful check is on slide 9: a $1,000 cap on added maintenance guarantees savings, but savings do not require that tight a cap.

For your next missed question, state the task in one sentence. Then explain which part of that task your selected answer failed.

Save the map for review. The full article and free guide are linked in the first comment.
```

**Slide 03**

Family 1, Identify the Parts: Role in Argument and Main Conclusion. Headline: Find the claim the author is ultimately defending. A three-step chain: Premise, gives support. Intermediate conclusion, receives support, then supports another claim. Main conclusion, where the support ends. The check: Take two candidate conclusions and ask which one the author uses as a reason for the other. On Role in Argument, find the main conclusion first, then locate the quoted statement relative to it. Watch for: A sentence does not become the main conclusion because it comes last or follows “therefore.” On Role questions, an answer can name the right role and still misstate what that statement supports. Slide 3 of 13.

**Slide 04**

Family 2, Describe the Argument: Method of Reasoning and Parallel Reasoning. Headline: Remove the topic. Describe the move. Text: State the move in plain language. The author might eliminate alternatives, compare two cases, apply a rule, or show that a proposed explanation conflicts with a fact. The check, read an answer as a set of claims. Sample answer: “The author cites an expert and questions that expert’s motives.” A green check marks Cites an expert. A red cross marks Questions that expert’s motives. If the author never questions motives, the answer fails. It started accurately and finished with a move the author never makes. Watch for: Matching the topic or familiar words. Two arguments about doctors can reason differently. For Parallel Reasoning, compare the conditions, the direction of support, and the force of the conclusion. Slide 4 of 13.

**Slide 05**

Family 3, Critique the Argument: Flaw and Parallel Flaw. Headline: Name the gap before you read the flaw answers. Text: Ask what the author needed to establish but left open. What could be true about the situation that would expose the weakness in the inference? The check, translate the answer back. On the left, the answer says: “The argument confuses a requirement with a guarantee.” An arrow points right to: You point to 1, the requirement in the stimulus, and 2, the claim that treats it as a guarantee. If you cannot locate that error in the reasoning, knowing the name of the fallacy is not enough. Watch for: A real flaw that this argument does not commit. In Parallel Flaw, the correct answer must fail in the corresponding way. Another bad argument is not necessarily a parallel bad argument. Slide 5 of 13.

**Slide 06**

Family 4, Change the Argument. Headline: Helpful, required, and sufficient are different standards. A table of what the answer must do. Evaluate: help you judge the reasoning. Weaken: reduce the support for the conclusion. Strengthen: improve the support for the conclusion. Sufficient Assumption: make the conclusion follow. Necessary Assumption: be something the argument requires. Principle Strengthen: be a general rule that helps justify the conclusion. Watch for: Using “this helps” as the standard for every question in the family. These six often begin from the same gap. The next four slides hold one argument fixed and change the task. Slide 6 of 13.

**Slide 07**

Family 4 worked example. Headline: Hold the argument still. Change the task. The argument, invented for teaching: Switching a library’s conventional lights to Model L fixtures will cut its annual electricity bill by exactly $4,000. All other annual operating costs stay unchanged, except that Model L may add maintenance costs. So the switch will reduce total annual operating costs. The gap, how much maintenance rises: a bar of added maintenance from $0 to $6,000, green below $4,000 and red from $4,000 up. Under $4,000: total costs fall and the conclusion holds. $4,000 or more: no reduction. Task 1, Evaluate: Ask how much Model L will add to annual maintenance. An answer of $1,000 supports the conclusion. An answer of $5,000 defeats it. Contrasting responses change the assessment. Slide 7 of 13.

**Slide 08**

Family 4 worked example. Headline: A weakener need not disprove. A strengthener need not prove. Context line: $4,000 saved; net change equals added maintenance minus $4,000. Task 2, Weaken: “Model L will add $5,000 a year in maintenance.” That more than cancels the $4,000 saving. This weakener happens to disprove the conclusion in this specified case. Weakening does not always require disproof. Task 3, Strengthen: “Three libraries with comparable operating hours and the same fixtures incurred maintenance increases under $1,000.” That supports a low estimate here. It does not guarantee the estimate, because this library’s conditions could differ. The check: Accept the answer as true. Then say what it does to the inference, not just to the topic. Slide 8 of 13.

**Slide 09**

Family 4 worked example. Headline: Required is not the same as enough. A chart of added annual maintenance from $0 to $6,000. A long blue bar from $0 to $4,000 is labeled Required: under $4,000. A short gold bar from $0 to $1,000 is labeled Enough: $1,000 cap. A dashed line at $3,000 falls inside the blue bar but outside the gold one. Task 4, Necessary Assumption: “Model L will add less than $4,000 to annual maintenance costs.” Negate it: $4,000 or more. Then total costs do not fall, so the argument requires this. Task 5, Sufficient Assumption: “Model L will add no more than $1,000 to annual maintenance costs.” Add it to the premises: at least $3,000 is saved, so the conclusion follows. It is not necessary. A $3,000 increase, the dashed line, would still allow savings. Slide 9 of 13.

**Slide 10**

Family 4 worked example. Headline: Connect the facts, the rule, and the conclusion. Task 6, Principle Strengthen: “Libraries that replace conventional lights with Model L add no more than $1,000 in annual maintenance.” A three-step chain: The facts, this library is switching from conventional lights to Model L. The rule applies, so added maintenance is capped at $1,000. The conclusion, total annual costs fall by at least $3,000. The check: A rule about a different fixture model would not apply merely because its result looks useful. Increased support is enough unless the question asks you to make the conclusion follow. Across all six tasks: A statement may meet more than one standard. Here, “less than $4,000” is both necessary and sufficient. The stem determines which property you need to establish. Slide 10 of 13.

**Slide 11**

Family 5, Form Your Own Argument. Headline: Supported is not guaranteed. Unknown is not false. Four relationships to the evidence. Possible, not a standard: The facts leave it open. A claim can be plausible without being supported here. Supported, Most Strongly Supported: You can point to its support in the information provided. Guaranteed, Must Be True: Try to make it false while the stimulus stays true. If you can, it is not guaranteed. Contradicted, Must Be False: It cannot coexist with the facts. Something never discussed may simply be unknown. Fill in the Blank: Name the blank’s job first. A conclusion needs support from what came before. A missing premise needs to do the work the surrounding argument requires. Slide 11 of 13.

**Slide 12**

Family 6, Argument-Adjacent. Headline: Check both sides of the relationship. Agree / Disagree, shown as Speaker A and Speaker B: Establish each speaker’s position on the exact statement in the answer. Silence is not disagreement. Principle: Apply, shown as rule to case: Check the case against each condition in the rule. Meeting most of a rule’s requirements is not meeting all of them. Principle: Generalize, shown as case to rule: Start with the case and find the rule it illustrates. A principle about the same subject can still misdescribe the case. Paradox, shown as fact plus fact: State what is surprising: we would expect this, but that happened. A correct explanation lets both facts remain true and makes their coexistence less surprising. Slide 12 of 13.

**Slide 13**

Dark blue closing slide, Use the map in review. Headline: “Wrong answer” is not a diagnosis. Text: Next time you review a miss, write three lines. A white worksheet with three blank lines: 1, Question type. 2, The task, in one sentence. 3, The standard my answer failed. A gold panel, A diagnosis you can use: “I picked something that strengthened the argument when I needed something the argument required.” That tells you what to change. ## Decisions

- The worked example is in the carousel. The original eight slides named the Family 4 tasks but never showed them working. Family 4 is where one gap feeds six different tasks, and the source already had a worked case for it, so slides 7 to 10 hold the library argument still and change the task. Evaluate sits with the gap on slide 7 because an Evaluate answer asks for exactly that number.
- One rule per slide, stated as the headline. Each family slide pairs a rule (“Find the claim the author is ultimately defending.”) with a check you can perform and a “Watch for” trap taken from the source’s wrong-answer patterns. The original slides gave definitions; these give the next action.
- Demonstrations instead of more text. A support chain (slide 3), an answer split into its claims (slide 4), a translate-back pair (slide 5), a threshold bar (slide 7) and a range chart (slide 9) carry the teaching that the original carried in sentences.
- The map shows the process. Families 1 to 5 are a connected route and family 6 is boxed as separate checks, following the source’s connected-process presentation. All 20 types sit in a two-column list so the slide is worth saving.
- A cover that opens on the mistake. “True, helpful, and still wrong.” is the concrete tension from the source’s first paragraph, and the three standards on the cover are the distinction the rest of the carousel teaches.
- A close you can use. Slide 13 is a three-line review worksheet built from the source’s review question (“What standard did my chosen answer fail?”), with the source’s own example diagnosis.
- Consistent link wording. The original slide 8 said the links were “in the caption” while the caption said “first comment”. Instagram captions don’t make links clickable, so the slide, caption and comment all point to the first comment.
- Wording kept where it worked. Rules, methods and traps are close to the source text. Edits were for length or for a cleaner line break, and none of them changes a distinction (helpful, required and sufficient; possible, supported, guaranteed and contradicted).
- Brand rules. Highlighted boxes are enclosed on all sides (full borders or fills, no side or top accent bars). Body text is 28px or larger and labels 22px or larger, with at least 64px of margin. Arrows, checks and the not-equal signs are drawn as inline SVG because the kit’s subset fonts lack those glyphs.
- Export robustness. In this environment the exported render sets text about half a percent narrower than the live page, so a line break sitting at the edge can come out differently in the PNG. I scanned every text block for breaks within 1.5% of the edge and reworded or pinned them (non-breaking spaces in “Model L” and “still wrong.”) so the exports match the preview.
