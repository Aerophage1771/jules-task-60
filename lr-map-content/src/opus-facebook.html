LSAT Logical Reasoning

Know the task before you judge the answer.

The argument · original teaching example

Replacing a library’s lights with Model L fixtures will cut its annual electricity bill by exactly $4,000. Every other operating cost stays the same, except that Model L may add maintenance costs.

ConclusionTotal annual operating costs will fall.

  1. Helpful

    Strengthen

    “Three libraries with comparable hours and the same fixtures saw maintenance rise less than $1,000.”

    That supports a low estimate here. It doesn’t guarantee one.

  2. Required

    Necessary Assumption

    “Added maintenance will cost less than $4,000 a year.”

    Negate it: $4,000 or more would mean no reduction.

  3. Sufficient

    Sufficient Assumption

    “Added maintenance will cost $1,000 a year or less.”

    That guarantees at least $3,000 in savings.
    It isn’t required: a $3,000 increase still allows savings.

Before you decide whether an LSAT answer is good, decide what it needs to do.

An answer can be true, helpful, or logically interesting and still miss the question. A statement that strengthens an argument might not be necessary to it. A necessary assumption might leave the conclusion unproven.

The image holds one argument fixed so you can see the difference. Replacing a library’s lights with Model L fixtures will cut its annual electricity bill by exactly $4,000, and maintenance is the only other cost that may change. The author concludes that total annual operating costs will fall.

Strengthen needs a fact that improves the support. If three comparable libraries with the same fixtures saw maintenance rise less than $1,000, that supports a low estimate here. It doesn’t guarantee one.

Necessary Assumption needs something the argument requires. Added maintenance has to cost less than $4,000 a year. Negate that, and $4,000 or more would mean no reduction.

Sufficient Assumption needs a statement that makes the conclusion follow. A $1,000 cap on added maintenance guarantees at least $3,000 in savings. The argument doesn’t need a cap that tight, though: a $3,000 increase would still allow savings.

Helpful, required, and sufficient are different standards. So are possible, supported, guaranteed, and contradicted. Sufficient, not required. Add it to the premises first: the bill falls by $4,000 and nothing else rises, so total costs fall. Then negate it: Model L adds some maintenance cost. If that cost is $3,000, total costs still fall by $1,000, so the argument never needed the stronger claim.

One more check: “Added maintenance will cost less than $4,000 a year” is the required answer in the image, and in this example it is also sufficient. A statement can meet more than one standard. The question stem tells you which one you need.
