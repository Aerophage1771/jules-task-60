The LR Question Type Map

Know the task before you judge the answer. Six families organize all 20 Logical Reasoning types, and each type sets its own standard.

By Germaine Washington
On this page

Different Logical Reasoning questions ask you to do different work on an argument. If you do not identify that work before evaluating the choices, you can pick an answer that is true, helpful, or logically interesting and still get the question wrong.

A statement that strengthens an argument might not be necessary to it. A necessary assumption might not be enough to prove the conclusion. An accurate description of a sentence might name its topic without identifying its role. These are differences in the task, and they should change how you approach the question.

My post on seven common Weaken formats gave you several tools for attacking an argument. This map places that work in the larger process. Before you can weaken an argument, you need to know what the author is claiming, what supports that claim, and what the support fails to establish.

I organize the 20 question types into six families. The first five follow the work of analyzing an argument and then drawing a conclusion yourself. The sixth covers questions involving two speakers, a rule and a case, or facts that seem difficult to reconcile.

The Six Families at a Glance

Each family comes with one question to answer before you read the choices. A choice can be true, helpful, or familiar and still fail the task, so start with the work the question requires.

FamilyQuestion typesAsk first
1. Identify the PartsRole in Argument, Main ConclusionWhat is each statement doing?
2. Describe the ArgumentMethod of Reasoning, Parallel ReasoningHow do the parts work together?
3. Critique the ArgumentFlaw, Parallel FlawWhere does the reasoning fail?
4. Change the ArgumentEvaluate, Weaken, Strengthen, Sufficient Assumption, Necessary Assumption, Principle StrengthenWhat must the answer do to the support?
5. Form Your Own ArgumentFill in the Blank, Most Strongly Supported, Must Be True, Must Be FalseWhat do the statements complete, support, guarantee, or rule out?
6. Argument-AdjacentAgree / Disagree, Principle: Apply, Principle: Generalize, ParadoxWhat does each side of the relationship establish?

The first five families connect. Identifying an argument’s parts helps you describe its reasoning. Describing the reasoning makes its flaw easier to locate, and that gap becomes the starting point for changing its support. The fifth family changes your role: you test what the given statements support or rule out.

That is not an instruction to perform every stage on every question. Identify the requested task first. The sixth family sits apart from the sequence because it concerns a relationship: between two speakers, between a rule and a case, or between facts that seem to conflict.

1. Identify the Parts

Question types: Role in Argument and Main Conclusion.

Start by figuring out what each statement is doing. On a Main Conclusion question, you need the claim the author’s reasoning is built to establish. A sentence does not become the main conclusion because it comes last or follows “therefore.” An intermediate conclusion receives support and then helps support another claim.

A useful check is to take two candidate conclusions and ask which one the author uses as a reason for the other. The claim doing the supporting is not the main conclusion. Follow the support until you reach the claim that receives support and is not used to support anything else.

On a Role in Argument question, identify the main conclusion first. Then locate the quoted statement relative to it. Is that statement evidence, an intermediate conclusion, a concession, an opposing view, or background?

Check both parts of the answer: the role it names and the claim it says the statement relates to. “A premise” might be the right role while the rest of the description misstates what that premise supports.

The mistake to avoid: choosing a statement because it is important or true. You are identifying its job.

2. Describe the Argument

Question types: Method of Reasoning and Parallel Reasoning.

Once you know the parts, describe how they work together. For Method of Reasoning, remove the topic and state the move in plain language. The author might eliminate alternatives, compare two cases, apply a rule, or show that a proposed explanation conflicts with a fact.

Then read each answer as a set of claims about the stimulus. If it says the author cites an expert and questions that expert’s motives, both things need to happen. An answer can start accurately and finish with a move the author never makes.

Parallel Reasoning asks you to find the same structure in a different argument. You might begin with a simple description, such as “a rule is applied to a case.” If several choices fit, compare the relationships more closely: the conditions, the direction of support, and the force of the conclusion.

The mistake to avoid: matching topics or familiar words. Two arguments about doctors can reason differently. An argument about a museum can use the same reasoning as one about a forest.

3. Critique the Argument

Question types: Flaw and Parallel Flaw.

Now ask whether the reasoning works. For Flaw, identify the specific problem before trying to recognize it in abstract answer wording. What did the author need to establish but leave open? What could be true about the situation that would expose the weakness in the inference?

Then translate each answer back into the stimulus. If an answer says the argument confuses a requirement with a guarantee, identify the requirement and the claimed guarantee. If you cannot locate that error in the reasoning, knowing the name of the fallacy is not enough. My complete LSAT flaw list can help you recognize the wording; the translation back into this argument is still the step that decides the question.

Parallel Flaw adds a matching task. The correct answer must fail in the corresponding way. Another bad argument is not necessarily a parallel bad argument.

The mistake to avoid: picking a familiar flaw because it is a real flaw. It must be the flaw this argument commits.

4. Change the Argument

Question types: Evaluate, Weaken, Strengthen, Sufficient Assumption, Necessary Assumption, and Principle Strengthen.

This is the largest family, at 37% of the 5,002 released Logical Reasoning questions in my explanation library (PrepTest 1 through 159). Its six types often begin from the same gap, the one you locate when you critique the argument. They ask you to do different things with it.

  • Evaluate: Find information that would help you judge the reasoning. Consider contrasting answers to the proposed question. Would they change your assessment of the argument?
  • Weaken: Find a fact that reduces the support for the conclusion. Preserve the given evidence and show why it may not establish what the author claims.
  • Strengthen: Find a fact that improves the support for the conclusion. Address the actual inference, rather than merely adding something favorable about the topic.
  • Sufficient Assumption: Add a statement that makes the conclusion follow. Put it together with the premises and check whether any gap remains.
  • Necessary Assumption: Find something the argument requires. Negate a candidate precisely and ask whether the reasoning still has the support it requires. You do not have to show that the conclusion itself becomes false.
  • Principle Strengthen: Find a general rule that helps justify the conclusion in this case. Connect the facts, the rule, and the conclusion. Increased support is enough unless the question asks you to make the conclusion follow.

One Argument, Six Tasks

The differences are easiest to see with the facts held still. This is an original teaching example. Read each sample answer below on its own, as the only new information you have.

Premises: Replacing a library’s conventional lights with Model L fixtures will reduce its annual electricity bill by exactly $4,000. All other annual operating costs stay unchanged, except that Model L may add maintenance costs.

Conclusion: The switch will reduce total annual operating costs.

Name the gap before you read any answer. The premises fix the saving and leave one number open: the added annual maintenance cost. Call it M. Total annual operating costs change by M minus $4,000, so the conclusion holds exactly when M is below $4,000.

Added annual maintenanceChange in total annual costsThe conclusion
$1,000Costs fall by $3,000.Holds.
$4,000Costs break even.Fails: there is no reduction.
$5,000Costs rise by $1,000.Fails.

Each sample answer below does something different with that threshold.

TaskSample answerWhat it does to the argument
EvaluateHow much will annual maintenance increase?A $1,000 response supports the conclusion; a $5,000 response defeats it. Contrasting responses change the assessment, which is what Evaluate requires.
WeakenModel L adds $5,000 a year in maintenance.That more than cancels the $4,000 saving, so this weakener disproves the conclusion in this case. Weakening does not always require disproof.
StrengthenThree libraries with comparable operating hours and the same fixtures saw maintenance rise by less than $1,000 a year.It supports a low estimate for this library. It does not guarantee that estimate, because this library’s conditions could differ.
Necessary AssumptionThe added annual maintenance cost is below $4,000.Negate it: $4,000 or more means no reduction. In this numerical example, the exact threshold is both necessary and sufficient. No added maintenance at all would be stronger than the argument requires.
Sufficient AssumptionAdded annual maintenance will be no more than $1,000.That cap guarantees at least $3,000 in savings. It is sufficient but not necessary: a $3,000 maintenance increase would still allow savings.
Principle StrengthenLibraries that replace conventional lights with Model L fixtures add no more than $1,000 in annual maintenance.The rule applies to this library and supplies a sufficient cap. A rule about another fixture model would not apply merely because its result looks useful.

All six answers work on the same gap, but that shared starting point does not make the tasks interchangeable. One statement can meet more than one standard, as the $4,000 threshold does here, and the question stem determines which property you need to establish. If the required standard is the one you tend to blur, Necessary Assumption Questions: Key Ingredients and Defenders works through it in detail.

The mistake to avoid: using “this helps” as the standard for every question in the family. Helpful, required, and sufficient are different.

5. Form Your Own Argument

Question types: Fill in the Blank, Most Strongly Supported, Must Be True, and Must Be False.

Here the task concerns what the statements support, complete, or rule out. On Most Strongly Supported, look for the claim with the best support in the information provided. You should be able to point to that support. A claim can be plausible in the real world without being supported by this stimulus.

Must Be True asks for a guarantee. Try to make the answer false while leaving the stimulus true. If you can do that, it is not required by the facts.

Must Be False asks for the opposite relationship: an answer that cannot coexist with the given information. Something the stimulus never discusses is not automatically false. It may simply be unknown.

Fill in the Blank requires another preliminary check: what is the missing statement doing? A conclusion needs support from what came before. A missing premise needs to perform the work the surrounding argument requires. Use the sentence and the question wording to identify that job before choosing a completion.

One Set of Facts, Four Relationships

The library shows these differences too. Keep both premises, set the conclusion aside, and add the Strengthen answer from the table above as a third fact: at three libraries with comparable operating hours and the same fixtures, maintenance rose by less than $1,000 a year. Then test four claims against those three statements.

ClaimRelationship to the factsWhat that means for the question
If Model L adds less than $4,000 in annual maintenance, total annual operating costs will fall.GuaranteedTry to make it false while the facts stay true. You cannot: the saving is exactly $4,000, maintenance is the only other cost that changes, and the claim’s condition keeps that change under $4,000. It would be a correct Must Be True answer.
The switch will probably reduce this library’s total annual operating costs.Supported, not guaranteedThe comparable libraries give you a reason to expect a small maintenance increase, but this library could differ. The claim can answer a Most Strongly Supported question. It fails Must Be True.
Model L fixtures cost more to buy than conventional lights.Possible, but unknownThe facts never address purchase price. The claim may sound plausible in real life, but here it is neither supported nor ruled out, so it is not a Must Be False answer either.
The switch will reduce the electricity bill by less than $4,000.ContradictedThe first premise fixes the saving at exactly $4,000, so this claim cannot coexist with the facts. It is the kind of answer Must Be False asks for.

The facts never changed across those four claims. Only each claim’s relationship to them did, and each question type asks for a different relationship.

The mistake to avoid: treating possible, supported, guaranteed, and contradicted as the same judgment. They are four different relationships to the evidence.

6. Argument-Adjacent

Question types: Agree / Disagree, Principle: Apply, Principle: Generalize, and Paradox.

These questions involve relationships that need their own checks. For Agree / Disagree, establish each speaker’s position on the exact statement in the answer. Silence is not disagreement. If one speaker’s view is unknown, you do not yet have the required relationship.

For Principle: Apply, break the rule into its conditions and result. Then check the case against each condition. Meeting most of a rule’s requirements is not meeting all of them.

Principle: Generalize reverses the direction. Start with the case and identify the broader rule or relationship it illustrates. A principle about the same subject can still misdescribe the case.

For Paradox, state what is surprising: “We would expect this, but instead that happened.” A correct explanation lets both given facts remain true and makes their coexistence less surprising.

The mistake to avoid: ignoring one side of the relationship. Check both speakers, the rule and the case, or both facts in the apparent conflict.

“Too strong,” “out of scope,” and “new information” are not automatic reasons to eliminate an answer. What counts as a defect depends on the task. A Sufficient Assumption may need a strong rule. Strengthen and Weaken invite new information. An inference may use different wording while remaining supported.

How to Use the Map in Practice

When you begin a question, run the same three steps before you commit to a choice.

  1. Identify the type from the question stem, then state the task in ordinary language.
  2. Choose the tool that task calls for. Sometimes that is a full prephrase. Sometimes it is an abstract structure. Sometimes it is a gap, a rule, or a contradiction you need to test against the choices.
  3. Hold each choice to the standard that task sets. Required does not mean sufficient, and supported does not mean guaranteed.

You do not have to predict the exact wording of every correct answer. You do need to know what work the answer has to do.

When you review a miss, use the same map to ask a more precise question:

What standard did my chosen answer fail?

“Wrong answer” is not much of a diagnosis. “I picked something that strengthened the argument when I needed something the argument required” tells you what to change. If your explanation stops at “out of scope” or “too strong,” make it more specific: what changed, and why does that change matter for this task? Knowing the task helps you justify the right answer, and it tells you exactly why the tempting alternatives fail.

Start with the next question you miss. Before you look at the choices again, write the standard from the table above in your own words. Then name the exact requirement your original answer failed.

If you can identify the question type but keep choosing answers that miss its standard, bring a recent example and your reasoning to a free consultation. We can examine where your process needs a more specific step.
