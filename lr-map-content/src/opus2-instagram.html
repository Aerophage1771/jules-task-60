Family 1 · Identify the Parts123456

Ask which claim the author uses as a reason for the other.

Role in Argument · Main Conclusion

Example stimulus

The clinic should hire a second nurse. Patients now wait more than an hour, so the current staff cannot meet demand.

PremiseLong waits
IntermediateStaff can’t meet demand
MainHire a second nurse

The shortage is a reason to hire, not the reverse. So the first sentence is the main conclusion, even though “so” comes later.

Watch for

An intermediate conclusion presented as the main one. On Role in Argument, check both parts of an answer: the role it names and the claim it says the statement supports.

Family 2 · Describe the Argument123456

Remove the topic.
Describe the move.

Method of Reasoning · Parallel Reasoning

A museum

Every museum in the district closes on Mondays. The Hale is one of them. So the Hale closes on Mondays.

A forest

Every trail in Pine Forest closes at dusk. Ridge Trail is one of them. So Ridge Trail closes at dusk.

Same move, different topics

A rule about a whole group, applied to one member of it.

Method of Reasoning: state that move in plain language. Parallel Reasoning: find it in a different argument. If several choices fit, compare the conditions, the direction of support, and the force of the conclusion.

Watch for

Matching topics or familiar words. Two arguments about doctors can reason differently.

04 / 14
Family 3 · Critique the Argument123456

Name the gap before you read the flaw answers.

Flaw · Parallel Flaw

Example stimulus

A deck must have a permit to pass inspection. Ana’s deck has a permit. So Ana’s deck will pass.

The gap

A permit is required to pass. Nothing says it is enough.

The answer says

“The argument treats a requirement as a guarantee.”

Point to both

Requirement: “must have a permit to pass”

Guarantee: “So Ana’s deck will pass.”

Watch for

A real flaw that this argument does not commit. In Parallel Flaw, the answer must fail in the corresponding way. Another bad argument is not necessarily a parallel one.

Family 4 · Worked example123456

One argument, six tasks. First, find what it leaves open.

The argument (invented for teaching)
Premise

Replacing a library’s conventional lights with Model L fixtures will cut its annual electricity bill by exactly $4,000.

Premise

All other annual operating costs stay the same, except that Model L may add maintenance costs.

Conclusion

So the switch will reduce the library’s total annual operating costs.

The gap: how much will maintenance rise?
Costs fall Costs rise
$0 added $4,000
breaks even $6,000

Net annual change = added maintenance minus $4,000

The conclusion holds only if added maintenance stays under $4,000. Each task on the next three slides works on that gap.

Family 4 · Worked example123456

Evaluate asks a question. Weaken adds a fact.

$4,000 saved · maintenance may rise · claim: total costs fall

$1,000: holds $5,000: fails
$0 $4,000
breaks even $6,000
EvaluateTask 1 of 6

“How much will Model L add to annual maintenance?”

If $1,000, the conclusion holds. If $5,000, it fails. Contrasting answers change the assessment, so the question is useful.

WeakenTask 2 of 6

“Model L will add $5,000 a year in maintenance.”

That more than cancels the $4,000 saving: total costs rise by $1,000. This weakener happens to disprove the conclusion. Weakening does not always require disproof.

Family 4 · Worked example123456

Strengthen adds support.
A principle must fit the case.

$4,000 saved · maintenance may rise · claim: total costs fall

StrengthenTask 3 of 6

“Three libraries with comparable operating hours and the same fixtures had maintenance increases under $1,000.”

That supports a low estimate here. It does not guarantee one, because this library’s conditions could differ.

Principle StrengthenTask 4 of 6

“Libraries that replace conventional lights with Model L add no more than $1,000 in annual maintenance.”

The factsThis library is making that switch
The rule appliesAdded cost is $1,000 at most
ConclusionCosts fall by $3,000 or more

A rule about a different fixture model would not apply merely because its result looks useful.

Family 4 · Worked example123456

Negate it to test necessary. Add it to test sufficient.

$4,000 saved · maintenance may rise · claim: total costs fall

Required: under $4,000 Enough: no more than $1,000 $0 $1,000 $3,000 $4,000 $6,000
Necessary AssumptionTask 5 of 6

“Model L will add less than $4,000 a year in maintenance.”

Negate it: $4,000 or more. Then total costs do not fall, so the argument requires it.

Sufficient AssumptionTask 6 of 6

“Model L will add no more than $1,000 a year in maintenance.”

Add it: at least $3,000 saved, so the conclusion follows. It is not necessary: a $3,000 increase (the dashed line) still saves $1,000.

Family 4 · Worked example123456

The stem decides which standard counts.

StrengthenSufficientNecessary

“Model L will add less than $4,000 a year.”

Yes Yes Yes

“Model L will add no more than $1,000 a year.”The answer on slide 1

Yes Yes No

“Three comparable libraries had increases under $1,000.”

Yes No No

A statement may meet more than one standard. The first one here is both necessary and sufficient. The $1,000 cap guarantees savings, but savings do not require that tight a cap.

The rule

Before you compare choices, state which property the stem asks for. Increased support is enough unless the question asks you to make the conclusion follow.

PossibleThe facts leave it open

Never discussed, so it is unknown.

Likely, since most are. Not guaranteed.

Try to make it false. You can’t.

ContradictedMust Be False

“No club member is a senior.”

It cannot coexist with the facts.

Fill in the Blank

Name the blank’s job first. A conclusion needs support from what came before; a missing premise must do the work the argument requires.

A statement can strengthen an argument without being necessary to it. It can be necessary without being enough to prove the conclusion.

If those standards blur together, the same answer can seem right for three different questions. Slide 1 shows one: a $1,000 cap on added maintenance meets the standard for Strengthen and for Sufficient Assumption, and fails it for Necessary Assumption.

I organized the 20 Logical Reasoning question types into six families. The first five follow the work you do on an argument: identify its parts, describe it, critique it, change it, and draw a conclusion yourself. The sixth covers two speakers, rules and cases, and apparent conflicts.

Slides 7 to 11 hold one library’s cost argument fixed and change the task six times. The Weaken examples gave you several ways to attack reasoning. This shows where that skill fits and what changes when the task changes.

For your next missed question, state the task in one sentence. Then explain which part of that task your selected answer failed.
