THE PROBLEM

A true answer can still be wrong.

  • A statement that strengthens an argument might not be necessary to it.
  • A necessary assumption might leave the conclusion unproven.
  • An accurate description of a sentence might name its topic without identifying its role.

These are differences in the task.

02 / 10

FAMILY 01 · IDENTIFY THE PARTS

What is each statement doing?

  • Role in ArgumentIdentify the job of the quoted statement.
  • Main ConclusionIdentify the claim the author is ultimately defending.

Check: Don’t choose a statement because it is important or true.

03 / 10

FAMILY 02 · DESCRIBE THE ARGUMENT

How does the reasoning work?

  • Method of ReasoningDescribe how the argument proceeds.
  • Parallel ReasoningMatch the reasoning structure.

Check: Don’t match topics or familiar words.

04 / 10

FAMILY 03 · CRITIQUE THE ARGUMENT

Where does the reasoning fail?

  • FlawIdentify the error in the reasoning.
  • Parallel FlawMatch the relevant flawed reasoning.

Check: Don’t pick a familiar flaw because it is a real flaw.

05 / 10

FAMILY 04 · CHANGE THE ARGUMENT

What happens to the support?

  • EvaluateFind information useful in assessing the argument.
  • WeakenReduce the support for the conclusion.
  • StrengthenImprove the support for the conclusion.
  • Sufficient AssumptionMake the conclusion follow when the answer is added.
  • Necessary AssumptionIdentify what the argument requires.
  • Principle StrengthenSupply a general rule that helps justify the conclusion.

Check: Helpful ≠ required ≠ sufficient.

06 / 10

FAMILY 05 · FORM YOUR OWN ARGUMENT

What do the facts establish?

  • Fill in the BlankComplete the passage in the role the blank requires.
  • Most Strongly SupportedIdentify the claim with the strongest support from the stimulus.
  • Must Be TrueIdentify a claim guaranteed by the statements.
  • Must Be FalseIdentify a claim incompatible with the statements.

Check: Don’t treat possible, supported, guaranteed, and contradicted as one judgment.

07 / 10

FAMILY 06 · ARGUMENT-ADJACENT

Check both sides.

  • Agree / DisagreeIdentify a shared or opposed position.
  • Principle: ApplyIdentify the case or judgment licensed by a rule.
  • Principle: GeneralizeIdentify the principle the case illustrates.
  • Paradox: Resolve, Reconcile, ExplainExplain how the surprising facts can coexist.

Check: Don’t ignore one side of the relationship.

08 / 10

PRACTICE

Name the task. Then test the choice.

1

State the task in one sentence before reading the choices.

2

After a miss, ask:

“What standard did my chosen answer fail?”

“Wrong answer” is not much of a diagnosis. “I picked something that strengthened the argument when I needed something the argument required” tells you what to change.

10 / 10
A statement can strengthen an argument without being necessary to it. It can be necessary without being enough to prove the conclusion.

If those standards blur together, the same answer can seem right for three different questions.

I organized the 20 Logical Reasoning question types into six families. The first five follow the work you do on an argument: identify its parts, describe it, critique it, change it, and draw a conclusion yourself. The sixth covers two speakers, rules and cases, and apparent conflicts.

The Weaken examples gave you several ways to attack reasoning. This map shows where that skill fits and what changes when the task changes. Swipe through all six families to see what each type asks you to do.

For your next missed question, state the task in one sentence. Then explain which part of that task your selected answer failed.
