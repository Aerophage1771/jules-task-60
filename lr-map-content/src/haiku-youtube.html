Purpose

Voiceover
Different Logical Reasoning questions ask you to do different work on an argument. If you do not identify that work before evaluating the choices, you can pick an answer that is true, helpful, or logically interesting and still get the question wrong. I organize the 20 question types into six families. The first five follow the work of analyzing an argument and then drawing a conclusion yourself. The sixth covers questions involving two speakers, a rule and a case, or facts that seem difficult to reconcile.

Family 1 of 6

Identify the Parts

What is each statement doing in the argument?

Role in ArgumentIdentify the job of the quoted statement.
Main ConclusionIdentify the claim the author is ultimately defending.

What not to do Choose a statement because it is important or true. You are identifying its job.

Scene 3 · 00:55 to 01:55

Purpose
Teach the first family: find the job of each statement before judging any choice.
Voiceover
Start by figuring out what each statement is doing. On a Main Conclusion question, you need the claim the author is ultimately trying to establish. A sentence does not become the main conclusion because it comes last or follows “therefore.” An intermediate conclusion receives support and then helps support another claim. On a Role in Argument question, identify the main conclusion first. Then locate the quoted statement relative to it. Is that statement evidence, an intermediate conclusion, a concession, an opposing view, or background? Check both parts of the answer: the role it names and the claim it says the statement relates to. “A premise” might be the right role while the rest of the description misstates what that premise supports. Do not choose a statement because it is important or true. You are identifying its job.

Family 2 of 6

Describe the Argument

How does the reasoning move? Does each answer describe that move?

Method of ReasoningDescribe how the argument proceeds.
Parallel ReasoningMatch the reasoning structure.

What not to do Match topics or familiar words. Two arguments about doctors can reason differently.

Scene 4 · 01:55 to 02:55

Purpose
Teach the second family: state the move in plain language, then test each answer as a set of claims.
Voiceover
Once you know the parts, describe how they work together. For Method of Reasoning, remove the topic and state the move in plain language. The author might eliminate alternatives, compare two cases, apply a rule, or show that a proposed explanation conflicts with a fact. Then read each answer as a set of claims about the stimulus. If it says the author cites an expert and questions that expert’s motives, both things need to happen. An answer can start accurately and finish with a move the author never makes. Parallel Reasoning asks you to find the same structure in a different argument. If several choices fit, compare the relationships more closely: the conditions, the direction of support, and the force of the conclusion. Do not match topics or familiar words. Two arguments about doctors can reason differently. An argument about a museum can use the same reasoning as one about a forest.

Family 3 of 6

Critique the Argument

Does the reasoning work, and what specific error would expose it?

FlawIdentify the error in the reasoning.
Parallel FlawMatch the relevant flawed reasoning.

What not to do Pick a familiar flaw because it is a real flaw. It must be the flaw this argument commits.

Scene 5 · 02:55 to 03:55

Purpose
Teach the third family: name the specific gap, then check that the choice describes this argument's error.
Voiceover
Now ask whether the reasoning works. For Flaw, identify the specific problem before trying to recognize it in abstract answer wording. What did the author need to establish but leave open? Then translate each answer back into the stimulus. If an answer says the argument confuses a requirement with a guarantee, identify the requirement and the claimed guarantee. If you cannot locate that error in the reasoning, knowing the name of the fallacy is not enough. Parallel Flaw adds a matching task. The correct answer must fail in the corresponding way. Another bad argument is not necessarily a parallel bad argument. Do not pick a familiar flaw because it is a real flaw. It must be the flaw this argument commits.

Family 4 of 6

Change the Argument

What would change the support for the conclusion?

EvaluateFind information useful in assessing the argument.
WeakenReduce the support for the conclusion.
StrengthenImprove the support for the conclusion.
Sufficient AssumptionMake the conclusion follow when the answer is added.
Necessary AssumptionIdentify what the argument requires.
Principle StrengthenSupply a general rule that helps justify the conclusion.

What not to do Use “this helps” as the standard for every question in the family. Helpful, required, and sufficient are different.

Scene 6 · 03:55 to 04:55

Purpose
Teach the fourth family: six tasks that start from the same gap but ask for different things.
Voiceover
These types often begin from the same gap. They ask you to do different things with it. Evaluate asks for information that helps you judge the reasoning. Weaken asks for a fact that reduces the support for the conclusion. Strengthen asks for a fact that improves that support. Sufficient Assumption asks for a statement that makes the conclusion follow. Necessary Assumption asks for something the argument requires. Principle Strengthen asks for a general rule that helps justify the conclusion in this case. Suppose the author offers evidence about how well a plan worked in one city and concludes that it will work in another. A difference between the cities may weaken the inference. A relevant similarity may strengthen it. That shared starting point does not make the tasks interchangeable. Do not use “this helps” as the standard for every question in the family. Helpful, required, and sufficient are different.

Family 5 of 6

Form Your Own Argument

What do the statements support, complete, or rule out?

Fill in the BlankComplete the passage in the role the blank requires.
Most Strongly SupportedIdentify the claim with the strongest support from the stimulus.
Must Be TrueIdentify a claim guaranteed by the statements.
Must Be FalseIdentify a claim incompatible with the statements.

What not to do Treat possible, supported, guaranteed, and contradicted as the same judgment.

Scene 7 · 04:55 to 05:55

Purpose
Teach the fifth family: keep possible, supported, guaranteed and contradicted apart.
Voiceover
Here the task concerns what the statements support, complete, or rule out. On Most Strongly Supported, look for the claim with the best support in the information provided. A claim can be plausible in the real world without being supported by this stimulus. Must Be True asks for a guarantee. Try to make the answer false while leaving the stimulus true. If you can do that, it is not required by the facts. Must Be False asks for an answer that cannot coexist with the given information. Something the stimulus never discusses is not automatically false. It may simply be unknown. Fill in the Blank asks what the missing statement is doing. Use the sentence and the question wording to identify that job before choosing a completion. Do not treat possible, supported, guaranteed, and contradicted as the same judgment. They are four different relationships to the evidence.

Family 6 of 6

Argument-Adjacent

How do two speakers, a rule and a case, or two facts relate?

Agree / DisagreeIdentify a shared or opposed position.
Principle: ApplyIdentify the case or judgment licensed by a rule.
Principle: GeneralizeIdentify the principle the case illustrates.
Paradox: Resolve, Reconcile, ExplainExplain how the surprising facts can coexist.

What not to do Ignore one side of the relationship. Check both speakers, the rule and the case, or both facts in the apparent conflict.

Scene 8 · 05:55 to 06:55

Purpose
Teach the sixth family: check both sides of every relationship before you judge it.
Voiceover
These questions involve relationships that need their own checks. For Agree / Disagree, establish each speaker’s position on the exact statement in the answer. Silence is not disagreement. If one speaker’s view is unknown, you do not yet have the required relationship. For Principle: Apply, break the rule into its conditions and result. Then check the case against each condition. Meeting most of a rule’s requirements is not meeting all of them. Principle: Generalize reverses the direction. Start with the case and identify the broader rule or relationship it illustrates. For Paradox, state what is surprising: “We would expect this, but instead that happened.” A correct explanation lets both given facts remain true and makes their coexistence less surprising. Do not ignore one side of the relationship. Check both speakers, the rule and the case, or both facts in the apparent conflict.

Worked example · setup

The argument we will test.

Premises

Replacing a library’s conventional lights with Model L fixtures will reduce its annual electricity bill by exactly $4,000.

All other annual operating costs stay unchanged, except that Model L may add maintenance costs.

The gap
Net annual cost change = M − $4,000

M is the annual maintenance increase. The conclusion holds exactly when M is below $4,000.

Conclusion

The switch will reduce total annual operating costs.

Worked example · part 2

Same argument, three tasks.

Evaluate

Ask how much annual maintenance will increase. A $1,000 increase supports the conclusion; a $5,000 increase defeats it.

Weaken

An added $5,000 annual maintenance cost more than cancels the saving.

Weakening does not always require disproof.

Strengthen

If three libraries with comparable operating hours and the same fixtures incurred maintenance increases under $1,000, that supports a low estimate here.

It does not guarantee the estimate.

Worked example · part 3

Same argument, three more tasks.

Necessary Assumption

The added annual maintenance cost must be below $4,000.

Negate that: $4,000 or more would mean no reduction.

Sufficient Assumption

A $1,000 cap on added annual maintenance guarantees at least $3,000 savings.

It is sufficient but not necessary.

Principle Strengthen

A general rule that libraries replacing conventional lights with Model L add no more than $1,000 in annual maintenance applies to this case and supplies a sufficient cap.

A $1,000 cap guarantees savings, but savings do not require that tight a cap.

Scene 11 · 08:10 to 08:50

Purpose
Show the assumption and principle tasks on the same gap, and make the sufficient-versus-necessary point.
Voiceover
Same argument, three more tasks. Necessary Assumption asks what the argument requires. The added annual maintenance cost must be below $4,000. Negate that: $4,000 or more would mean no reduction. Sufficient Assumption asks for a statement that makes the conclusion follow. A $1,000 cap on added annual maintenance guarantees at least $3,000 savings. It is sufficient but not necessary: a $3,000 maintenance increase would still allow savings. Principle Strengthen asks for a general rule. Take this one: libraries replacing conventional lights with Model L add no more than $1,000 in annual maintenance. That rule applies to this case and supplies a sufficient cap. A $1,000 cap guarantees savings, but savings do not require that tight a cap.
