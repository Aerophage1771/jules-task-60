# Use the connected map

The arrows show relationships between skills. Identifying an argument’s parts helps you describe its reasoning; describing the reasoning makes its flaw easier to locate; that gap becomes the starting point for changing its support. The fifth family changes your role: you test what the given statements support or rule out.

This is not an instruction to perform every stage on every question. Identify the requested task first. The sixth family sits separately because it concerns a relationship between speakers, between a rule and a case, or between surprising facts.

All 20 types are retained. No frequency percentages are needed to use the map.

## The five connected families

1. **Identify the Parts:** Role in Argument and Main Conclusion. Find what each statement does and what the author is ultimately defending.
2. **Describe the Argument:** Method of Reasoning and Parallel Reasoning. Describe or match how the premises support the conclusion.
3. **Critique the Argument:** Flaw and Parallel Flaw. Identify the error or match that faulty reasoning.
4. **Change the Argument:** Evaluate, Weaken, Strengthen, Sufficient Assumption, Necessary Assumption, and Principle Strengthen. Find useful information, change support, or supply the required kind of connection.
5. **Form Your Own Argument:** Fill in the Blank, Most Strongly Supported, Must Be True, and Must Be False. Complete the missing job or determine what the statements support, guarantee, or rule out.

## The separate sixth family

**Argument-Adjacent:** Agree / Disagree compares two speakers on the same claim. Principle: Apply moves from rule to case. Principle: Generalize moves from case to the general rule it illustrates. Paradox explains how the surprising facts can coexist. These are separate relationship tasks, not later steps after the main trail.

### Readable text

# Choose the standard before judging the answer

## Role in Argument

**Required:** The cited statement’s actual job and relationship.

**Check:** Find the main conclusion. Trace what the statement supports, is supported by, or contributes as context.

**Watch for:** A correct role paired with the wrong claim.

## Main Conclusion

**Required:** The claim the author is ultimately defending.

**Check:** Compare candidate claims. Ask which is used as a reason for another, then follow the support.

**Watch for:** An intermediate conclusion that supports a further claim.

## Method of Reasoning

**Required:** An accurate description of the author’s reasoning moves.

**Check:** Describe the move without the topic. Locate every claimed action in the stimulus.

**Watch for:** A partly accurate answer that adds a move never made.

## Parallel Reasoning

**Required:** The relevant reasoning structure must match.

**Check:** Abstract the pattern. Compare its conditions, direction, and conclusion’s force.

**Watch for:** Familiar vocabulary or topic hiding different logic.

## Flaw

**Required:** An error the argument actually commits.

**Check:** Identify the gap. Translate each criticism back into the stimulus’s terms.

**Watch for:** A real fallacy that is absent from this argument.

## Parallel Flaw

**Required:** The corresponding faulty reasoning must match.

**Check:** Name the original error. Check that the answer makes the same faulty move.

**Watch for:** Another bad argument with a different flaw.

## Evaluate

**Required:** The information must help assess the inference.

**Check:** Name the gap. Try contrasting responses and explain how the assessment changes.

**Watch for:** An interesting question whose answers leave the inference untouched.

## Weaken

**Required:** The new fact must reduce support for the conclusion.

**Check:** Accept the answer as true. Explain what becomes less secure about the inference.

**Watch for:** A negative fact about a side issue rather than the argument’s support.

## Strengthen

**Required:** The new fact must increase support for the conclusion.

**Check:** Find the gap. State what the new fact adds to the evidence already given.

**Watch for:** Repeating a premise or adding favorable but irrelevant information.

## Sufficient Assumption

**Required:** Premises plus the answer must establish the conclusion.

**Check:** Identify the missing connection. Add the answer and try to leave the conclusion unproven.

**Watch for:** A partial bridge, or the right terms connected in the wrong direction.

## Necessary Assumption

**Required:** The reasoning must require the answer.

**Check:** Negate a serious contender accurately. Check whether the reasoning can still work.

**Watch for:** A helpful or sufficient statement that is not required.

## Principle Strengthen

**Required:** An applicable general rule must help justify the conclusion.

**Check:** Match the rule’s conditions to the case, then connect its result to the conclusion.

**Watch for:** A rule that would help if its unestablished conditions were met.

## Fill in the Blank

**Required:** The completion must perform the missing statement’s job.

**Check:** Use the context and stem to identify that job. Check the proposed completion in place.

**Watch for:** A relevant-sounding statement that does not complete the reasoning.

## Most Strongly Supported

**Required:** The answer must have the strongest support in the facts.

**Check:** Combine relevant statements. Identify the evidence for the claim and any added assumptions.

**Watch for:** A plausible claim the stimulus does not support.

## Must Be True

**Required:** The answer must hold whenever the given statements hold.

**Check:** Try to make the answer false while keeping the stimulus true.

**Watch for:** A claim that could fail without contradicting the facts.

## Must Be False

**Required:** The answer cannot coexist with the given statements.

**Check:** Add the choice to the facts and locate the contradiction.

**Watch for:** Treating an unknown or unsupported claim as false.

## Agree / Disagree

**Required:** Both speakers must hold the required relationship to one claim.

**Check:** Establish each speaker’s position on that exact statement.

**Watch for:** One clear position and one speaker’s silence.

## Principle: Apply

**Required:** The case and judgment must conform to the rule.

**Check:** Check the rule’s direction and conditions. Confirm that it licenses the proposed judgment.

**Watch for:** Meeting only some conditions, or reaching an unlicensed result.

## Principle: Generalize

**Required:** The case must illustrate the proposed rule.

**Check:** Abstract the case’s actors, conditions, action, and result; match the relationship.

**Watch for:** A rule about the topic that misstates the case’s relationship.

## Paradox

**Required:** The explanation must help the surprising facts coexist.

**Check:** Name the expectation and surprising result. Preserve both facts while testing the explanation.

**Watch for:** Denying a fact or explaining an unrelated detail.

Strong wording, new information, and unfamiliar vocabulary are not automatic reasons to reject an answer. Test the specific claim against the task.

### Readable text

# One Argument, Six Different Tasks

This is an original teaching example. Each proposed answer is considered separately.

**Premises:** Replacing a library’s conventional lights with Model L fixtures will reduce its annual electricity bill by exactly $4,000. All other annual operating costs stay unchanged, except that Model L may add maintenance costs.

**Conclusion:** The switch will reduce total annual operating costs.

Let M be the annual maintenance increase. The net annual cost change is M minus $4,000. The conclusion holds exactly when M is below $4,000.

## Evaluate

Ask how much annual maintenance will increase. A $1,000 increase supports the conclusion; a $5,000 increase defeats it. Contrasting responses change the assessment.

## Weaken

An added $5,000 annual maintenance cost more than cancels the saving. This weakener actually disproves the conclusion in the specified case. Weakening does not always require disproof.

## Strengthen

If three libraries with comparable operating hours and the same fixtures incurred maintenance increases under $1,000, that supports a low estimate here. It does not guarantee the estimate, because this library’s conditions could differ.

## Necessary Assumption

The added annual maintenance cost must be below $4,000. Negate that: $4,000 or more would mean no reduction. In this numerical example, this exact threshold is both necessary and sufficient. Necessity and sufficiency are properties that can overlap.

## Sufficient Assumption

A $1,000 cap on added annual maintenance guarantees at least $3,000 savings. It is sufficient but not necessary: a $3,000 maintenance increase would still allow savings.

## Principle Strengthen

A general rule that libraries replacing conventional lights with Model L add no more than $1,000 in annual maintenance applies to this case and supplies a sufficient cap. A rule about another model would not apply merely because its result looks useful.

## Your next question

State the task before comparing choices. Then write what the chosen answer actually does to the reasoning. A statement may meet more than one standard; the stem determines which property you need to establish.

### 1. One argument. Six different jobs.

A worked teaching example will hold one argument fixed and compare Evaluate, Weaken, Strengthen, Necessary Assumption, Sufficient Assumption, and Principle Strengthen.

### 2. The argument we will test.

The library expects an exact 4,000-dollar annual electricity saving; maintenance is the only annual operating cost that may increase. It concludes that total annual operating costs will fall.

### 3. Name the gap precisely.

The net cost change equals added maintenance minus 4,000 dollars. An increase below 4,000 produces savings; an equal increase breaks even; a larger increase raises total costs.

### 4. Evaluate: what would you ask?

Evaluate asks for the amount of added maintenance. Answers of 1,000 dollars and 5,000 dollars lead to opposite assessments of the cost-reduction conclusion.

### 5. Weaken: make the support worse.

An added 5,000-dollar annual maintenance cost exceeds the guaranteed 4,000-dollar electricity saving, so annual costs rise by 1,000 dollars and the conclusion is undermined.

### 6. Strengthen: add support.

Maintenance experience at three comparable libraries supports a lower maintenance estimate, strengthening the argument without guaranteeing this library’s actual costs.

### 7. Necessary: what must hold?

The maintenance increase must be below 4,000 dollars. Its negation, 4,000 or more, rules out a reduction. No maintenance increase at all is stronger than required.

### 8. Sufficient: make it follow.

A maintenance increase capped at 1,000 dollars guarantees at least 3,000 dollars in savings, but that cap is not necessary because a 3,000-dollar increase would also permit savings.

### 9. Principle: connect rule to case.

A general rule about libraries installing Model L applies to this case and supplies the 1,000-dollar cap. A rule about a different fixture model would fail because its trigger is not established.

### 10. Test the job, not the label.

Summary distinguishes the six question tasks and emphasizes that one statement can meet multiple standards; correctness depends on the requested task.
